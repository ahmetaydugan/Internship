## Day 5 - The 2012 Revolution, Visualizing Experiments, and the Definition of Intelligence:
On the fifth and final day of my third internship week, I focused on bringing together everything I had learned into a 15-minute presentation. Today was about clearly articulating the technological shifts of the 2010s and proving them with my own experimental data.

1. The 2012 Revolution: Three Pillars of AlexNet
In my presentation, I detailed what exactly changed in 2012 to make AlexNet possible. I explained the perfect storm of three critical factors: the maturation of deep algorithmic architectures, the availability of massive labeled datasets like ImageNet, and the sheer parallel processing power of GPUs. Presenting this triad helped me communicate how AI transitioned from an academic niche to an industry powerhouse, completely rewriting the rules of the field.

2. Data-Driven Proof: CPU vs. GPU and TensorBoard
To back up the historical context with hard evidence, I shared the results of yesterday's practical experiments. I presented the drastic time difference between CPU and GPU training, alongside the TensorBoard graphs showing how adding or removing layers directly affected the loss curve. Showcasing my own metrics and visual graphs rather than just quoting textbook facts made the presentation feel much more grounded and professional.

3. The Bonus: Deep Blue vs. AlexNet
For the final part of my presentation, I tackled a philosophical bonus question: What is the fundamental difference between Deep Blue (1997) and AlexNet (2012)? I argued that Deep Blue is often not considered true "intelligence" because it relied on brute-force search and hardcoded human rules to rapidly evaluate millions of positions. In contrast, AlexNet actually "learned" to recognize visual patterns from raw pixels without explicit human programming. This marked the absolute boundary between merely calculating outcomes and genuinely learning from data.

This presentation perfectly wrapped up my third week. I now feel incredibly confident not just in coding neural networks, but in understanding the historical context, the hardware realities, and the philosophical milestones that make them possible.
